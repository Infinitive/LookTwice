Love lives in everyday moments. 

Cooking dinner together. Cleaning the apartment and bedroom. Putting up the Christmas tree. Ordering cards. Baking cookies and trying not to make a mess. Grocery shopping. Caring for each other through illness. Driving the entire way to and from Wichita because travel makes us anxious sometimes. 
This is where marriage actually lives.

It lives in maintenance. Not maintenance as grim obligation, but as the repeated care required by anything meant to last.

A home becomes a home through accumulation. He cooks dinner. I clean the apartment. We make picadillo, pasta bowls, or breakfast. We run errands, repot lilies, buy towels because the old ones have holes, and come home with dirt, pots, and a can of spray paint for a project. We plan holiday travel, dread the drive, take the trip anyway, and return grateful for our own bed.

I often say that quality time can look boring to other people. Going out, cooking, spending the day together, watching Star Trek, lying quietly together, or completing the usual errands do not create a dramatic story. They create stability.

That word matters. Stability is not the absence of intensity. It is the structure that allows intensity to exist without destroying everything around it.

Marriage has taught me that love is not proven by how strongly I feel during a crisis. It is proven by whether I can remain present before the crisis begins. Whether I listen when the answer is no. Whether I notice another person's needs without making them fight for scraps of my attention. Whether I follow through after saying all the correct words.

I have failed at those things. I am honest about that too. There are times where the person beside me became a background character while I disappeared into a phone, a project, or my own internal world. There are times where shame made me defensive and defensiveness made repair harder. Love does not erase those failures. Love makes them consequential.

It also gives me a reason to keep changing.

But it is my husband and I together who made our house a home.

That is the scale at which devotion becomes visible. A made bed. A cooked meal. A clean room. A patient drive. Sitting nearby while one person works. Holding each other as sleep arrives. Giving grace after a bad day without pretending the bad day did not happen.

Love that lasts has to survive Tuesdays. It has to survive fatigue, budgets, errands, school stress, dishes, sick days, and the weeks when neither person has anything interesting to report.

The extraordinary part is not escaping ordinary life.

The extraordinary part is building one together.
