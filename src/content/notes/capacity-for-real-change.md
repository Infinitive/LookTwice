I wrote this sentence after one of the ugliest entries in my journal:

I should never forget my capacity for real change, no matter how impossible it may seem.

It was not written from confidence. It came after pages of self-accusation, fear, and the belief that I had repeated the same mistakes so many times that repetition had become identity. I was looking at the distance between the husband, student, and man I wanted to be and the person I believed I had proven myself to be. The gap felt permanent.

That is when the sentence appeared.

The reason it matters is not that it sounds hopeful. Hope can become another mood that disappears when circumstances change. The sentence matters because it makes a factual claim.

I have changed before.

My life contains evidence: enlisting and leaving Kansas; surviving diagnosis and medical retirement; using the GI Bill to start and continue school; moving from crisis into treatment; returning to therapy; rebuilding routines after they collapse; recognizing that substances and sleep deprivation worsen the very patterns I am desperate to escape; staying sober for meaningful stretches even after believing I could not; learning that a relationship can survive conflict only when both people keep showing up for the repair.

None of this happened because one morning I finally became disciplined. There was no grand moment where everything clicked. That may be the more useful truth.

Change has looked like stop and go. It has looked like progress followed by relapse, honesty followed by avoidance, a clean apartment followed by disorder, a good semester followed by academic panic. It has looked like making a system meant to support my life, then hyperfixating on the system so completely that I ignored the life beside me.

That does not make the change fake. It makes the change incomplete.

Hopelessness has a selective memory. In a bad state, the mind presents every failure simultaneously and removes all counterevidence. Being sober for over two weeks disappears. Going to therapy every Wednesday disappears. Returning to class disappears. Cleaning the apartment, cooking, caring for the dogs, finishing assignments, and having the difficult conversation all disappear. The current pain becomes the only available fact.

Reality corrects that distortion.

Reality shows a man repeatedly convinced that nothing will improve and repeatedly taking another step anyway. It shows the same goals appearing across months because they matter and because they are difficult. Stability. Presence. School. Health. Sobriety. A peaceful home. A marriage in which words and actions agree.

The repetition can feel humiliating. How many times can a person promise to change before the promise becomes meaningless?

The answer is that the promise alone is meaningless. The next action is not.

One present day is evidence. One completed assignment is evidence. One moment of listening without defensiveness is evidence. One evening with the phone down is evidence. One honest admission before the situation becomes a crisis is evidence. These actions are small enough to feel inadequate, but a life is made almost entirely of inadequate actions repeated until they accumulate.

I do not need to believe that transformation will be total or permanent. I need to remember that the person I am describing as fixed has already changed many times.

The record does not let me call myself hopeless without contradiction.

That is what the sentence is for.
