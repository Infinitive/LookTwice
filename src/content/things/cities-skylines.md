Before Public Affairs, there was Cities: Skylines.

I spent hours building cities and then doing something that may matter more: watching them move. Traffic flowed through roads I had drawn. Neighborhoods developed. Transit lines filled. Commercial centers emerged. A city became legible as thousands of small interactions produced by a few large design decisions.

The game changed how I looked at real places.

Cities often present themselves as facts. A highway is simply there. A neighborhood has the density it has. A bus route either exists or does not. Cities: Skylines makes the decision underneath the fact visible. Roads, zoning, transit, services, taxes, and public space produce behavior. **The built environment is not neutral and it is not inevitable.**

That realization helped lead me toward city planning and Public Affairs as a pathway to that. I became interested not only in what had been built but in the capacity to build differently. The beauty was not a perfect skyline. It was human coordination made visible: people creating systems large enough to support lives they would never personally know.

The game also satisfied the part of me that builds elaborate internal worlds. As a child, imagination created distant lands and fantasy histories. Here, imagination became infrastructure. The question was no longer only what a place looked like. It was whether the place worked.

Star Trek offers a future shaped by improved institutions. Cities: Skylines lets me experiment with the smaller decisions that might produce one.

The world is not only what is.

*It is also what can be built.*
